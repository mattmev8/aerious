    The problem

    Traffic that reads the headline and leaves

    Name the pain before the cure

    A reader who recognises their own situation in the first screen keeps reading. Describe the problem precisely and without exaggeration; the credibility you earn here pays for every claim below.

    Short pages assume the visitor already wants it

    They do not. For anything unfamiliar or expensive, a two-paragraph page leaves every doubt intact.

    Features lists do not change minds

    A list tells what the product has. It never says what the visitor’s week looks like after buying.

    The sales call carries the whole weight

    When the page does not persuade, a human has to, one conversation at a time. That does not scale.

    The solution

    One page that makes the whole argument, in order

    Sell the mechanism

    Explain why the solution works, not only that it works. A reader who understands the mechanism trusts the result and stops looking for the catch.

    Problem, then mechanism, then proof

    The order matters more than the length. Each block answers the question the previous one raised.

    Headlines that read as a summary

    Skimmers get the pitch from the H2s alone; readers get the detail underneath.

    One offer, repeated at the right moments

    After the promise, after the proof and at the end. Same action, same wording.

    Benefits

    What the buyer gets, stated as outcomes

    Each benefit below is written the way a customer would describe it to a colleague, not the way a spec sheet would.

    Fewer sales calls that start from zero

    Prospects arrive having read the argument. The call confirms; it does not convince.

    Objections handled at 3 a.m.

    Price, time, fit and risk are answered on the page, in the order they come up.

    A structure you can measure

    Scroll depth per section tells you where the argument loses people. Fix that block, not the whole page.

    Copy that survives redesigns

    The order and the headlines carry the sale; visuals can change without rewriting the pitch.

    FAQ

    The questions that stop people from buying

    Answered here so the page, not a salesperson, handles them. The same answers feed a FAQ rich result in search.

    When should a landing page be long instead of short?

    When the decision is expensive, unfamiliar or emotional: a course, a service, a considered purchase. Short pages work for offers people already understand. Length is not the goal; answering every question before it is asked is.

    Does anyone read a page this long?

    Buyers do, and skimmers scan the headlines. That is why every section here has a title that carries the argument on its own. Read only the H2s and you still get the pitch.

    How many calls to action should a sales page have?

    One offer, repeated. This page asks for the same action after the hero, after the proof and at the end. Different offers on one page split attention and lower conversion.

    Where do price and guarantee go?

    After the value is clear and the proof has landed. Price before benefits feels expensive; a guarantee right after the price removes the last excuse to wait.

    How do I adapt this page to my product with AstroWind?

    Every block is a widget in src/components/widgets (Hero, Content, Features, Steps2, Stats, Testimonials, Pricing, FAQs, CallToAction). Keep the order, replace the copy and images, wire the buttons to your checkout or form.

    Are the numbers and testimonials on this page real?

    The numbers are constraints, not results, and the testimonials are labelled placeholders. Use only real customer quotes and measured results on your own page; invented proof is the fastest way to lose the sale.
